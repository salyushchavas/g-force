Any organization that keeps client records, takes payments online or runs on email needs someone thinking about security. That is as true for a small accounting firm as it is for a bank, which is why cybersecurity offers so many career paths and rewards people who keep learning.

If you are considering the field, you probably have three questions: which roles exist, what skills they need, and how to land the first one. This guide answers each, then sets out a realistic route in.

## Five roles worth knowing

### Security analyst

For many people, this is the first dedicated security job. Analysts watch networks and systems for suspicious activity, investigate alerts and help put security controls in place. Many work in a security operations centre (SOC), sorting real threats from false alarms.

<p class="lead-in">Key skills</p>

- Firewalls, VPNs and intrusion detection systems
- Encryption and data protection
- Patient, careful analysis: most alerts turn out to be harmless, and the one that is not matters

### Penetration tester

Penetration testers, also called ethical hackers, are hired to break in with permission, so weaknesses are found before a real attacker finds them. Then they write reports explaining each weakness and how to fix it. The role suits people who enjoy puzzles and can write for a non-technical reader.

<p class="lead-in">Key skills</p>

- Scripting in Python and Bash
- Penetration testing tools and methods
- A deep understanding of network security

### Security engineer

Security engineers design and build the defences: network segmentation, secure configurations, access controls and the monitoring that analysts rely on. They also review new systems before launch, so security is part of the design rather than a patch added later.

<p class="lead-in">Key skills</p>

- Network architecture and secure protocols
- Firewalls, encryption and VPNs
- Designing and testing security controls

### Incident responder

When a breach happens, incident responders work out what happened, contain the damage and help restore systems. It is a high-pressure role that rewards calm, methodical people. Between incidents, they write response plans and rehearse them with the teams involved.

<p class="lead-in">Key skills</p>

- Incident response procedures and digital forensics
- Threat detection and containment
- Clear thinking and clear communication under pressure

### Security consultant

Consultants assess an organization's security, recommend improvements and help put them in place, often for several clients across different industries. Most come to consulting after years in hands-on roles.

<p class="lead-in">Key skills</p>

- Assessing and reducing risk
- Explaining findings to owners and managers
- Privacy and compliance requirements, such as PIPEDA, Canada's federal privacy law for businesses

## A realistic route in

Security analyst postings usually ask for some IT experience, so many people start one step earlier.

Help desk work is not a detour. You learn how accounts, devices and networks behave, and you see first-hand how phishing emails and reused passwords cause real problems. Study for a foundation certification while you work, build a small home lab to practise on, and volunteer for security tasks such as reviewing who has access to what or rolling out multi-factor authentication. Those tasks become the examples you talk about in your first security interview.

## Certifications at each stage

Certifications do not replace experience, but they help your résumé get past the first screen. A sensible order:

- **Starting out:** CompTIA Security+ covers the core concepts and appears in many entry-level postings.
- **Specializing in testing:** Certified Ethical Hacker (CEH) focuses on attack techniques and tools.
- **Senior and leadership roles:** CISSP is aimed at experienced practitioners. You need several years of paid security work to hold it in full, and ISC2, which runs it, [publishes the exact requirements](https://www.isc2.org/certifications/cissp).

A cloud certification such as AWS Certified Cloud Practitioner or Microsoft Azure Fundamentals is a useful addition, since so much of what you will protect now runs in the cloud. For free Canadian guidance on what small organizations should have in place, read the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security's material at [cyber.gc.ca](https://www.cyber.gc.ca).
