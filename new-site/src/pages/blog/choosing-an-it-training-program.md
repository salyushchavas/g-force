There is no shortage of IT training: college certificates, bootcamps, online platforms, vendor courses and private trainers. Most promise a new career. Fewer explain what you will be able to do at the end, or what happens after the last class.

Choosing well matters, because a program costs more than tuition. It costs evenings, weekends and months of attention. Here is how to compare your options, starting with the question that makes every other decision easier.

## Start from the job you want

Before you compare programs, write down the job you are aiming for. Software developer, network administrator, security analyst, cloud engineer and IT project manager need different skills, and a program built for one will not prepare you for another.

If you are not sure yet, read a dozen job postings for roles that interest you. [Job Bank](https://www.jobbank.gc.ca), the Government of Canada's job site, is a good place to start, alongside the large job boards. Note the tools, certifications and experience that come up again and again. That list becomes your yardstick for every program you look at, and our article on [the IT skills Canadian employers are hiring for](/blog/it-skills-canadian-employers-hire-for/) can help you narrow it down.

## Check the curriculum against real postings

Technology changes quickly, and course outlines do not always keep up. Ask for the full outline, not the marketing page, and compare it with your list. Cloud computing, artificial intelligence and cybersecurity move especially fast, so check that the tools taught are the ones employers use now.

A good sign is a program that names specific platforms and tools, such as AWS or Azure, Python, or a particular security toolset, and tells you which certification exams it prepares you for. An outline made of broad topics with no tools named usually means a broad, shallow course.

## Pick a format you can keep up with

The best program is the one you finish. Be honest about your week:

- **Virtual classes** give you a live instructor and a fixed schedule from anywhere, which helps if you need structure.
- **In-person workshops** suit people who learn best with an instructor beside them during labs.
- **Self-paced lessons** fit around shift work or a full-time job, but you need the discipline to keep going without a class to show up for.

Some programs let you mix formats, for example self-paced lessons with live sessions for questions. If you work shifts, ask whether recordings are available when you miss a class.

## Insist on hands-on work

In IT, knowing the theory is not enough. Employers want to know you have done the work: built a network, configured a firewall, deployed an application, cleaned up a messy dataset. Look for labs, simulations and projects that mirror real tasks, and ask how much of the course time is hands-on.

Projects you finish during the course also give you something to show in interviews. A short write-up of a lab you built says more than a certificate on its own.

## Ask what happens after the last class

A course ends; your job search does not. Ask whether the program includes mentoring, career guidance, help with your résumé and interviews, or introductions to employers, and how long that support lasts. Be cautious of any program that guarantees you a job. No training provider controls an employer's hiring decision.

## Questions to ask before you enrol

Put these to any provider, including us:

- What job titles do your graduates usually apply for?
- Can I see the full course outline and the list of labs?
- Which certification exams does the program prepare me for, and are exam fees included?
- Who teaches it, and have they worked in the field?
- How much of the course is hands-on?
- What support do I get after the course ends, and for how long?
- What is the total cost, including materials and exams?

Clear, specific answers are a good sign. Vague ones tell you something too.
